# PARV4 Website — Priority Additions Draft

## Purpose

This document proposes the **next priority content to add to the PARV4 scientific knowledge website**. It is deliberately focused on additions that substantially improve scientific value rather than simply increasing the amount of text.

The public website should continue to follow one rule:

> **Every scientific statement must have a traceable public scientific reference.**

The user's unpublished observations are **not included** in this document or the public evidence base.

---

# Priority 1 — PARV4 and Acute Encephalitis Syndrome (AES)

## Why this should be the highest-priority addition

The published literature contains repeated Indian observations of PARV4 in AES/encephalitis, including direct detection in CSF and recovery of complete or near-complete genomes.

The website should therefore have a dedicated page:

**`PARV4 and AES: Evidence Review`**

### Suggested structure

1. Discovery of PARV4 in encephalitis
2. Bellary study
3. Complete genomes from northern India
4. Gorakhpur AES study
5. Other neurological reports
6. Case-control evidence
7. CSF versus serum/plasma findings
8. Viral-load observations
9. Genotypes identified
10. Alternative pathogens/co-infections
11. Evidence supporting causality
12. Evidence against/limitations
13. What remains to be demonstrated

### Key published evidence to include

- PARV4 was detected in CSF from **2 of 12 children** investigated for CNS infection in the 2011 Indian study.
- One of those children had approximately **1.5 × 10^7 copies/mL PARV4 DNA in CSF** and approximately **5.6 × 10^9 copies/mL in serum**.
- A second child had approximately **3.2 × 10^5 copies/mL in CSF**.
- Complete genome sequences were generated from the neurological cases and classified as genotype 2.
- A later northern Indian study generated complete/near-complete genomes from AES-associated specimens.
- The Gorakhpur study detected PARV4 DNA in **20/78 (25.6%) CSF** and **6/31 (19.3%) plasma** samples from AES patients and classified three almost-complete genomes and 24 partial NS1 sequences as genotype 2A.
- Approximately 70% of the Gorakhpur cohort showed serological evidence suggestive of *Orientia tsutsugamushi* infection, which is an important limitation when interpreting causality.

### Critical conclusion

The site should **not** state that PARV4 causes AES.

Recommended wording:

> **PARV4 has been repeatedly detected in Indian AES/encephalitis investigations, including directly in CSF and as complete or near-complete genomes. These findings support a potential association but do not yet establish PARV4 as an etiological agent of AES.**

### References

- Benjamin et al., 2011.
- Prakash et al., 2015.
- Gorakhpur AES study, 2019.
- Matthews et al., 2017.

- VP1
- VP2
- promoters

- terminal regions
- known/unknown genome features
- GenBank reference genomes

### Protein table

| Protein | Main evidence | Current interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| NS1 | Experimental | Nonstructural protein; associated with replication/cell-cycle effects |
| VP1 | Experimental | Structural capsid protein |
| VP2 | Experimental | Major capsid component; forms VLPs |
| Other predicted products | Limited | Function incompletely characterized |

A molecular characterization study demonstrated PARV4 transcription and expression of NS1a, VP1 and VP2; experimental NS1a expression induced G2/M arrest in ex vivo human hematopoietic stem cells. This should be presented as experimental protein biology, **not automatically as a mechanism of natural infection**.

### Important "known vs unknown" box

**Known**
- approximately 5-kb ssDNA genome
- major NS and VP coding regions
- three recognized human genotypes

**Unresolved**
- complete terminal structure
- natural receptor
- productive replication system
- complete life cycle
- natural cellular tropism

# Priority 5 — Diagnostic Interpretation

Create:

**`How to Interpret a PARV4 Positive Result`**

This should become a practical reference page.

## Possible result patterns

### PCR positive / high viral load

Compatible with active systemic infection, but clinical significance depends on specimen and context.

### PCR positive / antibody positive

Could represent acute or recent infection, depending on timing.

### Tissue PCR positive / plasma negative

May be compatible with persistence.

### CSF PCR positive / serum positive

Requires evaluation of:
- systemic viremia
- blood contamination
- true CNS infection

### CSF PCR positive / serum negative

Potentially stronger evidence for compartmentalized CNS infection, but requires confirmation and biological evidence.

## Recommended evidence package for suspected CNS disease

1. Quantitative PCR
2. Paired CSF and serum/plasma
3. Serial sampling
4. IgM/IgG and/or seroconversion
5. Complete/near-complete genome sequencing
6. Exclusion of alternative pathogens
7. Evidence of local CNS immune response where available
8. Independent confirmation by targeted PCR

## Human PARV4

Confirmed human host.

## Related primate viruses

Relevant to evolutionary comparison.

## Ungulate tetraparvoviruses

Related viruses, not established reservoirs of human PARV4.

## Bat/rodent/didelphimorph tetraparvoviruses

Evolutionary relatives; no demonstrated role in human PARV4 transmission.

### Important statement

> **No animal reservoir or intermediate host for human PARV4 has been established.**

# The most important architectural recommendation

Do **not** create each webpage independently.

Build a structured literature/evidence database first.

The relationship should be:

`Publication`
→ `Study`
→ `Observation`
→ `Evidence statement`
→ `Website section`
→ `In-text citation`

For example:

`Gorakhpur 2019 paper`
→ `78 AES CSF samples`
→ `20 PARV4 positive`
→ `25.6%`
→ `PARV4 & AES page`
→ `[Reference ID]`

This will prevent inconsistencies between pages and make future updates much easier.

# Editorial rule

For every statement displayed publicly:

### 1. What was observed?
Cite the primary study.

### 2. What did the authors conclude?
Cite the same study and clearly identify it as the authors' interpretation.

### 3. What does the total literature suggest?
Cite multiple studies/reviews.

### 4. What remains uncertain?
State the uncertainty explicitly.

### 5. What is not known?
Do not fill the gap with inference.
