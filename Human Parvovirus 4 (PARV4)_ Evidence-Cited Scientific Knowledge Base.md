# Human Parvovirus 4 (PARV4)

## A comprehensive reference on taxonomy, genomics, epidemiology, transmission, hosts, pathogenesis, clinical disease, diagnostics, immunity, vaccines and therapeutics

---

## 1. Overview

Human parvovirus 4 (PARV4) is a small, non-enveloped, single-stranded DNA virus belonging to the family *Parvoviridae*, subfamily *Parvovirinae*, and genus *Tetraparvovirus* [1–3]. The virus was first identified in 2005 during sequence-independent investigation of plasma from individuals presenting with an acute viral infection syndrome [1].

Current International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) classification places PARV4 in the species *Tetraparvovirus primate1*. The species contains three recognized PARV4 genotypes, G1, G2 and G3, as well as a chimpanzee-associated virus [3]. The genus *Tetraparvovirus* currently contains six recognized species and includes viruses associated with primates, bats, cattle, pigs, sheep, rodents and didelphimorphs [3].

PARV4 has an unusual epidemiological pattern. In several European and North American populations, infection is strongly associated with parenteral exposure, including injection drug use, haemophilia and exposure to plasma-derived products [4–6]. In contrast, studies from sub-Saharan Africa have demonstrated substantial infection in the general population and in children without recognized parenteral exposure, suggesting that transmission routes other than blood exposure may occur [7,8].

The clinical significance of PARV4 remains unresolved. Published studies have reported associations with encephalitis and acute encephalitis syndrome (AES), severe acute respiratory illness, influenza-like illness, fetal disease and other clinical conditions, but a definitive PARV4-specific disease syndrome has not been established [9–14].

PARV4 has been detected in blood, plasma, lymphoid tissues, bone marrow, liver and other tissues, and PARV4-specific cellular immune responses can persist for years following infection [15–18]. However, the biological significance of long-term viral DNA detection remains uncertain because productive replication and a complete infectious culture system have not been established [3,15].

**Current evidence status:** Human infection with PARV4 is established. Its natural transmission ecology, cellular tropism, replication biology, persistence and pathogenic potential remain incompletely defined [3,19].

# 2. Taxonomy and nomenclature

## 2.1 Current classification

| Taxonomic level | Classification |
|---|---|
| Family | *Parvoviridae* |
| Subfamily | *Parvovirinae* |
| Genus | *Tetraparvovirus* |
| Species | *Tetraparvovirus primate1* |
| Virus | Human parvovirus 4 (PARV4) |

PARV4 is the exemplar virus of *Tetraparvovirus primate1*. ICTV recognizes three principal PARV4 genotypes: G1, G2 and G3. Representative genotype sequences listed by ICTV are AY622943 (G1), DQ873390 (G2) and EU874248 (G3) [3].

The three genotypes show relatively limited amino-acid divergence, with ICTV reporting less than 3% amino-acid sequence divergence among the recognized genotypes [3].

### Historical names

The virus was originally designated PARV4. A related virus identified in plasma pools used for manufacture of plasma-derived products was initially designated PARV5 and is now recognized as PARV4 genotype 2 [2,7].

### Important nomenclature distinction

Human PARV4 should **not** be referred to as "ungulate tetraparvovirus." The ungulate tetraparvoviruses are separate species within the genus *Tetraparvovirus* [3].

---

# 3. Discovery

PARV4 was discovered in 2005 using sequence-independent PCR amplification and limited sequencing of plasma samples from 25 individuals presenting with symptoms of acute viral infection following high-risk behaviour for HIV transmission [1]. A previously undescribed parvovirus was identified in one of the samples, and nearly complete genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that it represented a previously unrecognized parvovirus lineage [1].

The initial report did not establish whether PARV4 was responsible for the clinical illness. The authors explicitly noted that the pathogenicity of the newly identified virus remained to be determined [1].

In 2006, Fryer and colleagues identified a related parvovirus in human plasma and plasma pools used in manufacture of plasma-derived medicinal products. This virus was initially designated PARV5 and was subsequently recognized as closely related to PARV4 and classified as genotype 2 [2,7].

The early association of PARV4 with injection drug use, HIV/HCV infection and plasma products led to the initial view that PARV4 was predominantly a blood-borne virus [4–6]. This interpretation was subsequently challenged by studies in sub-Saharan Africa that demonstrated infection in children and adults without recognized parenteral exposure [7,8].

---

# 4. Virion and genome

PARV4 is a small, non-enveloped virus with a single-stranded DNA genome of approximately 5 kb [3,20]. Like other parvoviruses, the viral capsid is composed principally of structural proteins VP1 and VP2 [20].

Current ICTV information indicates that PARV4 genomes are approximately 5 kb and that definitive terminal sequence information remains unavailable [3]. Consequently, the complete terminal organization and some aspects of the replication mechanism remain unresolved [3].

The major coding regions include:

- **NS region**, encoding nonstructural proteins including NS1;
- **VP region**, encoding capsid proteins VP1 and VP2;
- additional conserved or predicted reading frames described in molecular studies [20,21].

The published molecular characterization of PARV4 identified two promoters controlling expression of nonstructural and structural transcripts, respectively. The study identified major proteins including NS1a, VP1 and VP2 [20].

---

# 5. Viral proteins

## 5.1 NS1

NS1 is the major nonstructural protein and is expected to have an important role in viral genome replication and regulation of the infected cell [20].

Experimental expression of PARV4 NS1a demonstrated an ability to induce G2/M cell-cycle arrest in ex vivo-generated human hematopoietic stem cells [20]. This observation demonstrates biological activity of NS1 but does not by itself establish that the same mechanism occurs during natural PARV4 infection [20].

## 5.2 VP1 and VP2

VP1 and VP2 are the structural capsid proteins [20]. Recombinant VP2 expressed in yeast can self-assemble into virus-like particles (VLPs), which have subsequently been used for serological and antigenic studies [21,22].

---

# 6. Genotypes and geographic distribution

## Genotype 1

G1 has been reported predominantly in Europe and North America [3,19].

## Genotype 2

G2 was historically called PARV5. It has been detected in Europe, North America, Asia and Brazil and has been reported frequently in Asian studies [3,19].

Indian clinical studies of AES and respiratory disease have predominantly identified genotype 2 viruses [9,10,13].

## Genotype 3

G3 was first recognized in individuals from sub-Saharan Africa and subsequently detected frequently in infants in Ghana [7,8]. In the Ghanaian infant study, PARV4 viremia was detected in 8.6% of children over the two-year observation period [8].

---

# 7. Global epidemiology

## 7.1 Western populations

The strongest early epidemiological evidence for PARV4 came from populations with parenteral exposure.

In a serological study of injection drug users, anti-PARV4 antibodies were detected in 67% of HIV-infected injection drug users and 33% of HIV-uninfected injection drug users, whereas all 50 non-parenterally exposed adult controls were seronegative [4]. Among haemophiliacs treated with non-virus-inactivated factor VIII or IX, 11/20 HIV-positive individuals and 4/15 HIV-negative individuals were seropositive, while almost all non-haemophiliac siblings were seronegative [4].

These findings provided strong evidence that parenteral exposure was an important transmission route in these populations [4].

Autopsy-based investigations similarly found PARV4 infection predominantly among people with parenteral risk factors, including injection drug users and individuals with haemophilia [5].

---

# 8. Epidemiology in sub-Saharan Africa

The epidemiology of PARV4 in sub-Saharan Africa differs markedly from that observed in many Western populations.

A multicountry serological study reported PARV4 VP2 antibody seroprevalence of approximately:

- 37% in Burkina Faso;
- 25% in Cameroon;
- 35% in the Democratic Republic of Congo;
- 20% in South Africa [7].

The authors interpreted these findings as evidence that PARV4 transmission in Africa could not be explained solely by parenteral exposure [7].

The discovery of genotype 3 in Africa further supported this interpretation [8].

---

# 9. PARV4 infection in children

Panning and colleagues investigated infants in Ghana after identifying genotype 3 in two adults without recognized parenteral exposure. PARV4 viremia occurred in 8.6% of infants over two years, demonstrating that infection was common in this population [8].

The authors noted that overt clinical illness was not apparent in the infected children, raising the possibility that infection in this setting may frequently be asymptomatic or associated with prolonged low-level viremia [8].

PARV4 genotype 3 sequences detected in several Ghanaian villages formed a local phylogenetic subclade, suggesting local circulation of a particular lineage [8].

---

# 10. Respiratory and fecal detection

PARV4 DNA has been detected in nasal and fecal specimens from children in Ghana. In one study, 8/961 nasal specimens and 5/943 fecal specimens were positive [23].

The authors proposed respiratory and/or fecal–oral transmission as possible explanations for these observations but emphasized that detection of viral DNA in nasal or fecal material does not by itself prove that the virus is transmitted through those routes [23].

Accordingly:

| Transmission route | Evidence status |
|---|---|
| Parenteral | **Strongly supported** |
| Blood/plasma products | **Strongly supported** |
| Respiratory | **Suggested, not proven** |
| Fecal–oral | **Suggested, not proven** |
| Vertical | **Possible, insufficient evidence** |
| Sexual | **Not established** |
| Arthropod vector | **No established evidence** |
| Animal-to-human | **Not demonstrated** |

---

# 11. Blood and plasma-derived products

PARV4 was detected early in plasma pools used to manufacture plasma-derived medicinal products [2].

A study of 175 historical factor VIII concentrate lots detected PARV4 sequences in 28 lots (16%). Viral loads in some positive products exceeded 10⁵ copies/mL, and both genotypes 1 and 2 were detected in two lots [24].

Most positive factor VIII concentrates were manufactured during the 1970s and 1980s [24]. The investigators stated that the significance of PARV4 contamination for viral safety and recipient transmission was uncertain [24].

A prospective study of 195 individuals with haemophilia found baseline PARV4 seroprevalence of 44%. During five years of follow-up, nine participants seroconverted, corresponding to an incidence of 1.7% per year. The newly infected individuals had relatively prolonged viremia, with a mean duration of approximately seven months, and weak/transient IgM responses [25]. The study also found evidence that PARV4 could be transmitted through plasma-derived clotting factors despite viral-inactivation procedures used for those products [25].

---

# 12. Tissue distribution and persistence

PARV4 DNA has been detected in several human tissues.

Manning and colleagues investigated bone marrow, lymphoid tissue and brain tissue from HIV-infected and HIV-uninfected individuals and demonstrated tissue-associated PARV4 DNA, providing evidence that PARV4 can persist outside blood [15].

Schneider and colleagues examined liver tissue from 87 individuals, most of whom were HIV-negative, and detected PARV4 DNA in 13 specimens (15%). Genotype 2 DNA was also detected in one of four paired bone marrow samples [16]. Serum samples from 40 individuals were negative despite detection of viral DNA in liver tissue, supporting the possibility of tissue persistence after systemic viremia has resolved [16].

The viral loads in liver tissue were low, not exceeding 100 copies per microgram of genomic DNA in that study [16].

A separate tissue study reported PARV4 DNA in several organs and suggested relatively frequent detection in liver and heart compared with some other tissues [17]. The authors proposed these tissues as candidates for further investigation, but detection of DNA does not establish productive replication [17].

### Important interpretation

**Detection of PARV4 DNA in tissue should not automatically be interpreted as active viral replication.**

Possible explanations include:

- persistent infection;
- residual viral DNA;
- low-level replication;
- latent infection;
- persistence in particular cell populations.

The biological status of tissue-associated PARV4 remains unresolved [15–19].

---

# 13. Cellular tropism and replication

The natural cellular tropism of PARV4 remains poorly defined.

A molecular characterization study demonstrated that PARV4 can express viral proteins in experimentally transfected 293 cells and identified two promoters controlling nonstructural and structural transcripts [20]. The same study showed that NS1a can induce G2/M cell-cycle arrest in ex vivo-generated human hematopoietic stem cells [20].

However, these experiments demonstrate activity of viral genes rather than natural productive infection of those cell types.

ICTV currently states that no tetraparvoviruses have been successfully isolated or propagated in culture and that definitive information about PARV4 genome termini remains unavailable [3].

Consequently, major unresolved questions include:

- natural cellular receptor;
- permissive cell types;
- entry mechanism;
- replication sites;
- genome replication mechanism;
- virion assembly;
- release mechanism.

---

# 14. Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of PARV4 remains incompletely understood [19].

A useful conceptual model is:

**Exposure → entry → primary replication → viremia → tissue dissemination → persistence/clearance → immune response → clinical disease**

Evidence is strongest for infection and viremia, whereas the downstream steps remain less well characterized [19].

Acute viremia has been documented in several epidemiological studies [8,25]. Tissue-associated viral DNA has been demonstrated after apparent systemic infection [15–17]. Strong and persistent cellular immune responses have also been documented [18,26].

However, there is currently insufficient evidence to define whether persistent PARV4 represents:

- productive chronic infection;
- controlled persistent infection;
- latent infection;
- or persistence of non-replicating viral DNA.

---

# 15. Immune response

## 15.1 Humoral response

PARV4-specific IgG responses have been detected using recombinant VP2 antigens and VLP-based assays [4,21,22].

In a study using recombinant VP2 VLPs, 16/170 individuals with acute respiratory disease were seropositive for PARV4 antibodies. Six were IgM-positive/IgG-positive, six were IgM-positive/IgG-negative and four were IgG-positive/IgM-negative [21]. Seven seropositive individuals were children aged 3–11 years without evidence of parenteral exposure [21].

These findings provided additional evidence that PARV4 infection occurs outside populations with recognized parenteral risk.

---

# 16. Cellular immunity

PARV4 infection induces strong cellular immune responses.

A study of 101 individuals exposed to blood-borne viruses detected high-frequency T-cell responses against multiple PARV4 NS peptides in approximately 26% of participants. Typical responses to NS peptide pools exceeded 1,000 IFN-γ spot-forming cells per million PBMCs in some individuals [26].

The authors interpreted the magnitude of the cellular immune response, together with detection of viral antigen/DNA in tissues, as compatible with persistent, controlled infection [26]. This interpretation remains a hypothesis rather than definitive proof of productive persistent infection.

Simmons and colleagues studied individuals undergoing acute PARV4 infection and demonstrated persistent functional CD8+ T-cell responses with an active, mature and cytotoxic phenotype. These responses remained detectable several years after infection [27].

The persistence and breadth of PARV4-specific T-cell responses have been proposed as potentially relevant to future vaccine development [27].

---

# 17. PARV4 and the central nervous system

The possible relationship between PARV4 and CNS disease is one of the most important unresolved areas of PARV4 research.

## 17.1 Initial Indian observation

Benjamin and colleagues investigated CSF from 12 children with acute CNS infection in Bellary, India. High-throughput pyrosequencing identified PARV4 DNA in CSF from two children with encephalitis of unknown etiology [9].

The investigators reported high PARV4 levels in the CSF of the two children and proposed PARV4 as a potential cause of encephalitis. However, the study was small and did not establish causality [9].

## 17.2 Complete genomes from AES cases

Prakash and colleagues subsequently reported complete genome sequences from two PARV4 isolates detected in CSF from patients with AES in northern India [10]. The authors explicitly stated that an association between PARV4 and human disease had not yet been established [10].

## 17.3 Gorakhpur AES study

A larger investigation was undertaken during seasonal AES outbreaks in Gorakhpur, India. Next-generation sequencing identified PARV4 sequences in a pooled CSF/plasma specimen. Targeted real-time PCR subsequently detected PARV4 DNA in:

- **20/78 (25.6%) CSF samples**
- **6/31 (19.3%) plasma samples**

from AES patients [11].

Three almost-complete genomes and 24 partial NS1 sequences were classified as genotype 2A [11]. Approximately 70% of patients showed IgM/IgG positivity suggestive of *Orientia tsutsugamushi* infection, creating an important potential confounder [11].

The authors concluded that the observed association between PARV4 and AES required further evaluation [11].

The 2017 comprehensive review concluded that important questions about PARV4 pathogenicity remained unanswered but identified the Indian encephalitis observations as evidence supporting further investigation of a possible relationship between PARV4 and encephalitis [19].

# 19. PARV4 and respiratory disease

PARV4 has also been investigated in respiratory illness.

A case-control study in India tested nasal and throat swabs from 241 patients with severe acute respiratory illness and 146 healthy controls. PARV4 was detected in 64/241 (26.55%) cases and 1/146 (0.68%) controls [12].

PARV4 DNA occurred alone in 44 cases and was co-detected with other respiratory viruses in 20 cases [12]. Genotyping of 23 high-viremia samples identified genotype 2 [12].

The authors reported a strong statistical association between PARV4 detection and SARI but explicitly raised the question of whether PARV4 was a pathogen or a bystander [12].

A subsequent study investigated 1,111 patients with influenza-like illness and 220 healthy controls. PARV4 was detected in 135/1,111 cases (12.15%) and 1/220 controls; sequencing of 20 isolates suggested predominance of genotype 2 [13].

These studies provide evidence of association but do not establish that PARV4 is the direct cause of respiratory disease.

---

# 20. Other reported clinical associations

## Fetal disease

PARV4 has been reported in investigations involving fetal hydrops and maternal-fetal infection [19]. However, the available evidence is insufficient to establish PARV4 as a cause of fetal hydrops.

## Hepatitis

PARV4 infection has been investigated in individuals with hepatitis C and other blood-borne infections. Some studies have reported liver-associated viral DNA and clinical abnormalities during acute infection [16,19,25].

## HIV/HCV disease

PARV4 is epidemiologically associated with populations infected with HIV and HCV, particularly where parenteral exposure is common [4,19]. However, whether PARV4 independently influences HIV or HCV disease progression remains unresolved [19].

---

# 21. Diagnostics

## 21.1 PCR and qPCR

PCR has been the principal method for detecting PARV4 DNA in blood, tissues, CSF and respiratory specimens [4,5,9,11–13].

Published assays have targeted conserved genomic regions and have also been developed for genotype discrimination [19,20].

## 21.2 Serology

Serological assays have used recombinant VP2 and VP2-derived VLPs to detect PARV4-specific IgG and IgM [4,21,22].

## 21.3 Metagenomic sequencing

PARV4 was originally discovered through a sequence-independent molecular approach [1].

Metagenomic sequencing has subsequently been used to identify PARV4 in clinical specimens, including CSF from AES patients [9,11].

Metagenomics provides several advantages:

- unbiased detection;
- identification of unexpected viruses;
- genome recovery;
- genotype determination;
- phylogenetic analysis;
- simultaneous assessment of co-infections.

However, metagenomic detection alone does not establish causality.

---

# 22. Interpretation of PARV4 PCR positivity

A positive PARV4 PCR result should be interpreted in the context of:

- specimen type;
- viral burden;
- timing of sampling;
- serological status;
- persistence;
- clinical syndrome;
- presence or absence of alternative pathogens.

This is particularly important because PARV4 DNA has been detected in tissues in individuals without evidence of active plasma viremia [16].

For investigations of possible CNS disease, the most informative evidence would ideally include:

1. quantitative viral detection;
2. paired CSF and serum/plasma;
3. serial samples;
4. seroconversion or IgM response;
5. genome sequencing;
6. exclusion of alternative pathogens;
7. evidence of local CNS infection or immune response.

---

# 23. Whole-genome sequencing and phylogeny

PARV4 genome sequencing has been used to characterize genotypes and investigate geographical and clinical relationships [8,10,11,19].

The two AES-associated complete genomes reported from northern India were the first PARV4 genome sequences reported from that region [10].

The Gorakhpur investigation subsequently identified three almost-complete genomes and 24 partial NS1 sequences, all classified as genotype 2A [11].

Phylogenetic analysis can therefore be used to investigate:

- genotype distribution;
- geographic clustering;
- temporal circulation;
- lineage persistence;
- relationships between clinical and non-clinical isolates;
- relationships with animal tetraparvoviruses.

---

# 24. Hosts and reservoirs

## 24.1 Human host

Humans are an established host for PARV4, based on repeated detection of viral DNA and antibodies in human populations and tissues [3,19].

## 24.2 Non-human primates

ICTV includes a chimpanzee-associated virus within *Tetraparvovirus primate1* [3].

The existence of related primate viruses is relevant to evolutionary analysis but does not demonstrate that PARV4 originated through a recent zoonotic event.

## 24.3 Ungulates

Closely related tetraparvoviruses have been identified in cattle, pigs and sheep [3,19].

## 24.4 Bats and other mammals

ICTV currently recognizes tetraparvovirus species associated with bats, rodents and didelphimorphs in addition to primates and ungulates [3].

### Reservoir status

**No animal species has been demonstrated to be a reservoir or intermediate host for human PARV4.**

Related tetraparvoviruses should therefore be considered **comparative evolutionary evidence**, not evidence of an established animal-to-human transmission cycle [3].

---

# 25. Evolutionary relationships

PARV4 is evolutionarily distinct from other well-known human parvoviruses such as parvovirus B19 and human bocaviruses [19,20]. It clusters within the *Tetraparvovirus* lineage together with related viruses infecting other mammals [3,19].

The broader host range of the genus raises questions about the evolutionary history and host adaptation of tetraparvoviruses. However, phylogenetic relatedness alone cannot establish the direction or timing of cross-species transmission.

---

# 26. Vaccine research

## Current status

**No licensed PARV4 vaccine is currently available.**

There is also no established protective animal challenge model that has enabled conventional vaccine efficacy studies.

## VP2 virus-like particles

Recombinant PARV4 VP2 expressed in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* can self-assemble into VLPs [21]. These particles have been successfully used as antigens for detection of PARV4-specific antibodies [21].

A later study generated monoclonal antibodies against yeast-produced PARV4 VP2 VLPs and identified at least three distinct antigenic sites. The study also demonstrated that selected VP2 antigenic regions could be replaced by heterologous peptide sequences, supporting the potential use of PARV4 VLPs as an antigen-presentation platform [22].

These findings demonstrate **vaccine-platform potential**, but they do not demonstrate protective efficacy against PARV4 infection.

### Major barriers to vaccine development

- uncertain disease burden;
- uncertain target population;
- unresolved correlates of protection;
- incomplete understanding of viral replication;
- absence of a robust culture system;
- lack of a validated challenge model;
- uncertain clinical significance.

---

# 27. Therapeutics

## Current status

**No PARV4-specific antiviral treatment has been established.**

The absence of a robust infectious culture system limits conventional antiviral discovery and drug-screening approaches [3,19].

Potential research targets could theoretically include:

- viral entry;
- capsid-receptor interactions;
- NS1-mediated replication;
- viral DNA synthesis;
- host factors required for replication;
- neutralizing antibodies.

These remain **research possibilities rather than established therapies**.

---

# 28. Blood safety

The detection of PARV4 in plasma-derived products and its ability to persist in blood have made it relevant to transfusion medicine [2,24,25].

Historical factor VIII products contained detectable PARV4 sequences in 16% of tested lots [24].

Subsequent prospective evidence indicated that PARV4 transmission could occur among haemophilia patients exposed to virally inactivated clotting-factor concentrates [25].

The clinical significance of transfusion-associated PARV4 infection remains incompletely defined.

A 2024 reconstruction of historical blood-product contamination further included PARV4 among blood-borne viruses identified in historical clotting-factor concentrates, illustrating the broader relevance of retrospective molecular surveillance of plasma-derived products [28].

---

# 29. Recent blood-donor evidence

A study published in 2025 investigated 400 healthy blood donors in Iran using real-time PCR and detected PARV4 DNA in 6/400 donors (1.5%) [29]. The investigators emphasized that the pathogenesis and clinical implications of PARV4 remain undefined [29].

This finding provides evidence that PARV4 can still be detected among apparently healthy blood donors and supports continued investigation of its prevalence and potential transfusion implications.

---

# 30. PARV4 in healthy populations

The presence of PARV4 in apparently healthy individuals is an important consideration when interpreting disease-association studies.

PARV4 antibodies have been detected in individuals without recognized parenteral risk factors [7,21].

Similarly, PARV4 DNA has been detected in healthy blood donors [29].

Therefore, detection of PARV4 in a clinical specimen cannot by itself establish that the virus caused the patient's illness.

For disease-causality studies, the relevant comparison is not simply:

> **PARV4-positive patient vs PARV4-negative patient**

but preferably:

> **PARV4 prevalence and viral burden in disease cases vs appropriate clinical and healthy controls.**

---

# 31. PARV4 and causality

The central unresolved question is:

> **Is PARV4 a human pathogen, an opportunistic pathogen, a disease modifier, or predominantly a persistent/bystander virus?**

Several models remain possible.

### Model 1 — Primary pathogen

PARV4 directly causes disease in a subset of infected individuals.

### Model 2 — Opportunistic pathogen

Disease occurs preferentially in particular immunological or physiological states.

### Model 3 — Cofactor

PARV4 modifies disease caused by another infectious agent.

### Model 4 — Persistent bystander

PARV4 is frequently detectable because of tissue persistence but is not responsible for the clinical syndrome.

### Model 5 — Context-dependent pathogen

Different genotypes, host populations or transmission routes may produce different clinical outcomes.

The current literature does not definitively distinguish among these models [19].

---

# 32. PARV4 and AES: what would establish causality?

The existing literature establishes an association between PARV4 and AES/encephalitis in several Indian investigations [9–11].

However, the following additional evidence would substantially strengthen a causal inference:

### 1. Enrichment in AES

PARV4 should occur significantly more frequently in AES patients than in appropriate controls.

### 2. CNS localization

PARV4 should be demonstrated preferentially or specifically in CSF or CNS tissue.

### 3. High local viral burden

A high viral load in CSF, especially in the absence of equivalent systemic viremia, would support CNS involvement.

### 4. Evidence of acute infection

Seroconversion, IgM, rising antibody titres or a characteristic temporal relationship would support recent infection.

### 5. Evidence of replication

Detection of replication-associated intermediates, viral antigen, or productive infection in CNS-derived cells would provide substantially stronger biological evidence.

### 6. Exclusion of alternatives

Established AES pathogens should be comprehensively excluded.

### 7. Reproducibility

The association should be demonstrated independently in multiple populations and geographic regions.

### 8. Biological mechanism

A plausible mechanism of CNS entry, cellular tropism and neurological injury should be demonstrated.

# 33. Evidence grading

The website should classify individual statements using an explicit evidence framework.

### Established

Directly demonstrated by robust primary evidence or supported by multiple independent studies.

### Strong evidence

Supported by substantial evidence but with meaningful unresolved limitations.

### Suggestive

Observed in one or more studies but requiring independent confirmation.

### Unresolved

Evidence exists but is insufficient or conflicting.

### Unknown

No convincing published evidence identified.

This framework is particularly important for statements about:

- transmission;
- persistence;
- CNS infection;
- neurological disease;
- respiratory disease;
- reservoirs;
- vaccine efficacy.

# 35. Current scientific position

The published evidence establishes that PARV4 is a genuine human infection and that infection occurs in geographically and epidemiologically diverse populations [3,19].

Parenteral transmission is strongly supported by studies involving injection drug users, haemophiliacs and plasma-derived products [4–6,24,25].

African studies demonstrate that PARV4 can also occur in populations without recognized parenteral exposure, including infants, suggesting additional transmission routes [7,8].

PARV4 DNA can persist in human tissues and is associated with substantial and long-lasting cellular immune responses [15–18,26,27].

The most important unresolved clinical question is whether PARV4 is involved in human neurological disease. Multiple Indian investigations have detected PARV4 in AES/encephalitis cases, including direct detection in CSF and recovery of complete or near-complete genomes [9–11].

Nevertheless, **PARV4 has not yet been conclusively established as an etiological agent of AES**. The major unresolved biological questions are CNS tropism, productive replication, neuroinvasion, disease mechanism and the distinction between causative infection and persistent viral detection [19].

# 39. Editorial rule for the PARV4 portal

The following rule should govern the entire website:

> **Every scientific statement must be traceable to a publicly accessible scientific reference.**

Information should therefore be separated into:

### Published observation

What the investigators actually measured or demonstrated.

### Authors' interpretation

What the investigators concluded or proposed.

### Evidence synthesis

What multiple publications collectively indicate.

### Unknown

A question for which adequate published evidence has not been identified.

### Unpublished information

Not included in the public knowledge base.

This distinction is particularly important for PARV4 because several observations—especially CNS detection, tissue persistence and respiratory detection—are biologically interesting but do not automatically establish causation.

# 40. Overall scientific summary

PARV4 is a human tetraparvovirus discovered in 2005 and currently classified within *Tetraparvovirus primate1* [1,3]. It has three recognized human genotypes with distinct geographical patterns [3].

Parenteral transmission is strongly supported in Western high-risk populations, while studies in sub-Saharan Africa demonstrate substantial infection in children and adults without recognized parenteral exposure, indicating a more complex transmission ecology [4–8].

PARV4 DNA has been detected in blood, plasma and multiple tissues, and persistent cellular immune responses have been documented years after infection [15–18,26,27].

No established animal reservoir, productive culture system, licensed vaccine or PARV4-specific antiviral treatment currently exists [3,19].

The strongest unresolved clinical question is the potential relationship between PARV4 and neurological disease. Multiple Indian investigations have independently detected PARV4 in AES/encephalitis patients, including direct detection in CSF and recovery of complete or near-complete viral genomes [9–11].

However, **the published evidence currently supports an association rather than definitive causation**. Demonstration of CNS replication, cellular tropism, neuroinvasion and a reproducible disease-specific association would be required to establish PARV4 as an etiological agent of AES.

The major scientific value of a dedicated PARV4 knowledge resource is therefore not simply to collect what is known about the virus, but to make the distinction between **demonstrated evidence, reported association, proposed mechanism and unresolved question** explicit for every major topic.
